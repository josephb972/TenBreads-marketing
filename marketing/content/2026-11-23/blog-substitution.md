# The substitute case you did not approve: how out-of-stock swaps raise your food cost

Status: draft. Owner publishes. Not posted.
Pillar: Unauthorized SKU substitution. Persona: Executive Chef / F&B Director (US, $).
Suggested meta description: When a distributor substitutes an out-of-stock item, the price can change without anyone approving it. Here is how it happens and a simple way to check.

It is 6:40 a.m. The delivery driver tells you the case of whole-muscle chicken you ordered is out of stock. He has a similar product on the truck. You need protein for lunch service, so you say yes and sign.

That yes was about the food. It was not about the price. The invoice that arrives later may bill the substitute at a different price than the item you ordered.

## What the numbers can look like

Illustrative example: you order an item contracted at $32.50 a case. It is out of stock. The substitute is billed at $41.80 a case. The difference is $9.30 a case.

Now spread that over a group. If 12 locations each take 6 substituted cases a week, that is 72 cases. At $9.30 over, that is about $670 a week, or about $34,800 a year. (Illustrative numbers, not a client result.)

## Why this happens

Most price agreements include price protection. If the distributor substitutes, the substitute should be billed at the contracted price of the item you ordered, or at a price you agreed to. In practice, the invoice often bills the substitute at its own list or spot price.

Three things make this easy to miss:

**The chef approves the product, not the price.** At the back door, you decide whether the substitute works. The driver does not have the contract price on the truck, and you do not have the invoice yet.

**The item code changes.** The substitute has a different item number. Any check that matches on item code will not find the original item on the invoice, so there is nothing to compare it to.

**The invoice goes to AP.** The person who saw the substitution is not the person who pays the bill. The invoice does not say "this was a substitution." It is just another line.

## What to look for on an invoice

When a line appears that you did not order, ask four questions:

1. What was the ordered item, and what is its contract price?
2. What is the substitute, and what price was billed?
3. Does the contract say substitutes are billed at the ordered item's price?
4. What is the difference per case, and how many cases were substituted?

If the billed price is above the contract price of the ordered item, and your contract has price protection, the difference may be owed back as a credit.

## A weekly routine for kitchens and AP

You do not need new software to start. You need two lists and a habit.

**Step 1: Log substitutions at receiving.** Have the receiving person write down the ordered item, the substitute, and the case count. A photo of the delivery note works.

**Step 2: Share the log with AP each week.** One short list per location. Ordered item, substitute, cases, date, invoice number if known.

**Step 3: Check each logged line against the invoice.** Compare the billed price to the contract price of the ordered item. Note the difference per case.

**Step 4: Send a claim for the difference.** Include the invoice number, the line, the contract price and the billed price. Cite the substitution or price protection clause in your agreement. Ask for a credit.

**Step 5: Track the credit.** Confirm it appears on the next statement. If it does not, follow up.

## Where the routine breaks down

The routine works for one location. Across 12 or 20 locations and 150 invoices a week, the log is incomplete, the notes are handwritten, and the checking takes hours that AP does not have. Substitutions also happen when the receiving person is new or busy, which is when the log is skipped.

Some substitutions are fine. A cheaper substitute billed at the right price helps you. The goal is not to refuse every substitute. The goal is to make sure the price follows your contract.
