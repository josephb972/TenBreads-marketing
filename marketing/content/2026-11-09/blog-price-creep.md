# Contract price creep: how $0.35 a pound disappears between renewals

Status: draft. Owner publishes. Not posted.
Pillar: Contract price creep. Persona: Ops Director / Head of Supply Chain.
Suggested meta description: How distributor prices drift above the contracted rate a few cents at a time, why it goes unnoticed, and a simple weekly check to stop it.

You negotiated the price master. You pushed on chicken, dairy and dry goods, and you signed. Then the invoices started arriving, and nobody on your team had the time to confirm they matched.

That gap between the contract and the invoice is where price creep lives.

## What creep looks like

Creep rarely arrives as a single jump. It looks like this: chicken breast is contracted at $3.80/lb. A few weeks later it is billed at $3.94. A few weeks after that, $4.15. Each step is small. Neither is big enough to trigger a call to the rep.

Add the steps and the picture changes. From $3.80 to $4.15 is 35 cents a pound, about 9% on that line.

## Why it goes unnoticed

Three things make creep easy to miss.

**The contract lives in a PDF.** The price sits in a signed document or a spreadsheet. The invoice sits in an inbox. Nobody holds both at the same moment.

**Volume hides the change.** A 12-unit group can receive well over a hundred invoices a week. AP is checking totals, tax and coding. Line-level unit prices on hundreds of items are not part of the routine.

**Reps have room to move.** Weekly price files, cost-plus formulas and "market adjustments" give a distributor plenty of ways to move a price without anyone calling it a breach. If you did not sign off, it is worth asking whether the contract allowed it.

## An illustrative example

These numbers are illustrative, not from any client.

A 12-unit group buys 300 lb of chicken breast per location each week. That is 3,600 lb across the group.

- At 15 cents over contract: 3,600 lb x $0.15 = $540 a week, about $28,000 a year.
- At 35 cents over contract: 3,600 lb x $0.35 = $1,260 a week, about $65,500 a year.

That is one protein from one distributor. Dairy, produce, oil and dry goods can drift in the same way.

## Why the usual fixes fall short

An annual price review catches drift only after twelve months of overpaying. Spot-checking the top ten items covers the lines you already watch, not the long tail. And asking the rep to "look into it" works only if you can bring invoice numbers, dates and the contracted rate.

The useful version of this check is boring: every invoice line, matched to the contracted unit price, every week.

## A weekly check you can run

1. **Keep one current price master.** Item code, pack size, unit of measure and contracted rate, with the effective date of each change.
2. **Match on item code, not description.** Descriptions change. Codes are more stable.
3. **Compare unit rate, not extended total.** Totals move with quantity. The unit rate shows the drift.
4. **Flag any variance, however small.** A 14-cent difference on a high-volume item matters more than a $20 difference on something you buy once a quarter.
5. **Log each variance with invoice number, date and contract reference.** A claim with those details is much harder to wave away.
6. **Track the claim until a credit note arrives.** A promised adjustment that never shows up on the statement is the same loss under another name.

## What to do when you find it

Take the invoice number, the line, the billed rate and the contracted rate to your distributor's claims or order desk. Cite the contract clause. Ask for a credit, not a verbal assurance. Then watch the next statement to confirm the credit appears.

If creep is repeating, raise it with the account manager as a pattern, not a one-off, and ask what process they use to apply contracted prices at invoicing.
