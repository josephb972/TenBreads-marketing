# Catch-weight billing: how gross weight quietly inflates your protein spend

Status: draft. Owner publishes. Not posted.
Pillar: Catch-weight discrepancies. Persona: Executive Chef / F&B Director.
Suggested meta description: How catch-weight errors turn into overcharges on meat, seafood and cheese, and what to check on the dock and on the invoice.

Every chef knows the feeling of lifting a case and thinking it feels light. The label says 40 lb. The invoice says 42. Which one did you pay for?

That gap is the core of catch-weight billing problems, and it is one of the easiest overcharges to miss because it hides inside a line that looks normal.

## What catch-weight means

Most grocery items are fixed weight. A case of canned tomatoes is the same every time. Meat, seafood and cheese are not. A case of boneless short rib is sold by the pound, and the actual weight varies from case to case. The distributor records the real weight, and you are billed on it.

That is fair, as long as the weight billed is the weight you received.

## Where the error comes in

There are two weights on a protein case. Gross weight includes packaging, ice, liquid and the box. Net weight is the product you can actually cook and sell. Your price per pound should apply to net weight.

Errors happen when the invoice uses gross, or when a nominal case weight is billed instead of the actual one. Both push the total up. Neither is obvious on the dock, because you are checking the case count and temperature, not the arithmetic.

## An illustrative example

These numbers are illustrative, not from any client.

A case of salmon fillet is contracted at $9.50/lb. The net weight is 40 lb. The invoice bills 42 lb.

- Correct charge: 40 lb x $9.50 = $380.00
- Billed charge: 42 lb x $9.50 = $399.00
- Difference: $19.00 on one case

Now scale it. If a 12-unit group receives 30 cases like this a week, the overcharge is $570 a week. Over 52 weeks that is $29,640. All from two pounds per case, on one item, with no single invoice looking wrong.

## Why chefs see it first, and why it still gets missed

You are the person on the dock. You see the case, the label and the scale. But the invoice goes to AP, often days later, as one of 150 or more documents that week. By then the delivery is in the walk-in and the label is in the bin.

AP sees a price per pound that matches the contract. They do not see that the pounds are wrong, because nothing on the invoice tells them what the net weight should have been.

So the discrepancy sits between two teams. Neither is doing anything wrong. The process just does not connect the two.

## What to check on the dock

You do not need a new system for this. A few habits help.

1. **Read the label weight.** Note the net weight printed on the case label, and compare it to the delivery note.
2. **Spot-weigh.** Pick a case or two per delivery on the scale, net of packaging. Photograph the scale and the label together.
3. **Flag the case, not the vendor.** Write the invoice number, item code and weights on the delivery note before the driver leaves.
4. **Pass it on the same day.** Send the photo and note to AP so the claim has evidence attached.

A claim with a photo and a signed note is far harder for a distributor to reject than a month-old question.

## What to check on the invoice

For AP and finance teams, look for these:

- Billed weight greater than the label or delivery note weight.
- Round numbers where you would expect decimals, which can mean a nominal weight was used.
- The same item billed at different weights per case across locations with no explanation.
- Unit of measure changes, such as a line priced per case but contracted per pound.

Checking this by hand on every protein line is the problem. It is repetitive, and it is exactly the work that gets pushed down the list.

## Why it is worth the effort

Protein tends to carry high per-pound prices. A small weight error multiplied by a high price per pound adds up quickly.

It also compounds with other leaks. Illustratively, a price that has crept up 14 cents a pound, billed on weight that is 5% too high, is two overcharges in one line.
